<p class="science-intro">
I want to understand what dark energy, dark matter and gravity really are, using the large-scale structure of the Universe. Euclid and the other Stage IV surveys will measure it with unprecedented precision, and that precision only pays off if our models, our software and our statistics are up to the task. My research sits at the intersection of these three things.
</p>

<h2><i class="fa-solid fa-atom"></i> Beyond ΛCDM: model extensions</h2>

<p>
ΛCDM describes our Universe remarkably well, yet we still do not know what dark energy is, whether general relativity holds on cosmological scales, or what set up the initial conditions. I coordinate the <strong>model extensions</strong> analysis for <strong>Euclid's first cosmology data release (DR1)</strong>, which tests dark energy, modified gravity and other extensions of the standard model with Euclid's first cosmological data. Before Euclid, I worked on inflation and on new observables such as the cross-correlation of gravitational waves with galaxy clustering.
</p>

<h2><i class="fa-solid fa-satellite"></i> Verification and validation of Euclid's performance</h2>

<p>
Before Euclid can tell us anything new about the Universe, we need to know that the full chain, from the theory predictions to the final constraints, is correct and that it delivers the performance the mission was designed for. I have spent much of the last decade on exactly that: developing, verifying and validating the Cosmology Likelihood for Observables in Euclid (CLOE), and forecasting what Euclid will achieve by the end of the mission. I led the CLOE inference and forecasts paper, and this work is what led us to build the <a href="{{ '/software/' | relative_url }}">cloe-org software ecosystem</a>.
</p>

<h2><i class="fa-solid fa-dice"></i> Statistics in cosmology</h2>

<p>
Cosmology is, at its heart, a statistical science: we only get one Universe to observe. I am interested in how we make robust inferences from it, from Bayesian model comparison and reconstructions to simulation-based inference and interpretable machine learning, and in how we quantify when a result is biased or in tension. My PhD thesis, <em>Playing dice with the universe</em>, was about exactly this, and it is still the thread that runs through my work.
</p>
